What are the types of data and the levels of measurement (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio)?

Descriptive Statistics 7 min read Difficulty: Beginner 1 of 40
Definition

Data is qualitative (categorical) — labels such as centre or course — or quantitative (numerical) — counts and measurements. Numerical data is discrete (countable: number of backlogs) or continuous (any value in a range: attendance %, salary).

The level of measurement decides which statistics are meaningful: nominal (names only, no order: centre) → mode, counts; ordinal (ordered, unequal gaps: grade A/B/C, feedback 1–5) → median, percentiles; interval (equal gaps, no true zero: temperature in °C, calendar year) → mean, differences; ratio (true zero: salary, study hours) → all of these plus ratios ("twice as much").

qualitative quantitative discrete continuous nominal ordinal interval ratio
Analogy
Think of a race. Bib numbers are nominal (just names), finishing positions are ordinal (1st beat 2nd, but by how much?), clock times of day are interval, and race durations are ratio: a 2-hour run really is twice a 1-hour run.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Data is either qualitative (labels) or quantitative (numbers that measure or count).
  2. 2Qualitative data is nominal (no order) or ordinal (ordered categories with unequal gaps).
  3. 3Quantitative data is discrete (countable) or continuous (any value in a range).
  4. 4Each level of measurement allows more operations and therefore more summary statistics.
  5. 5Decide the level from meaning, not from the column's dtype.
Real-World Scenario
Choosing the right summary for each column

The academic report for a batch shows the most common centre (mode), the median feedback rating (ordinal), and mean attendance and package (ratio). Averaging feedback codes or centre IDs would produce numbers that look precise but mean nothing.

Cross Questions
Is a roll number numeric data?
No. It is stored as digits but it is nominal: adding or averaging roll numbers is meaningless.
Can you take the mean of a 1–5 Likert rating?
Strictly it is ordinal, so median and percentages per level are safest; in practice means of multi-item scales are common but should be reported with care.
Why is temperature in °C interval and not ratio?
0 °C is not "no temperature", so 20 °C is not twice as hot as 10 °C. Kelvin has a true zero and is ratio.
Is "placed (1/0)" quantitative?
It is a binary nominal variable, but coding it as 0/1 makes its mean equal the proportion placed, which is very useful.

Explain mean, median and mode. When is the median better than the mean?

Descriptive Statistics 7 min read Difficulty: Beginner 2 of 40
Definition

Mean = sum ÷ count; it uses every value, so a few extreme values pull it. Median = the middle value of the sorted data (average of the two middle values for an even count); it is robust to outliers. Mode = the most frequent value; the only one that works for nominal data, and data can be bimodal.

For symmetric data mean ≈ median. For right-skewed data (salaries, house prices, waiting times) the mean is pulled above the median, so the median better describes a typical value. A trimmed mean (drop the top and bottom x %) is a compromise. Weighted means account for different group sizes or credits.

mean median mode trimmed mean weighted mean robust
Analogy
Ten friends earn ₹50,000 a month each. A billionaire walks into the café: the mean income of the room becomes enormous, but the median person in the room still earns ₹50,000.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Placement packages are right-skewed: most offers are 4–8 LPA, a few are much higher.
  2. 2The mode sits at the peak, the median splits the area in half, and the mean is pulled furthest into the tail.
  3. 3Adding a single 60 LPA offer lifts the mean by a quarter of a lakh; the median is unchanged. The trimmed mean drops the extremes first.
  4. 4The mode is the only centre measure that works for categories.
  5. 5To combine group averages, weight each by its group size.
Real-World Scenario
Reporting placement packages honestly

A brochure that reports only the mean package can be lifted by a single 40 LPA offer. Institutions that want a fair picture report the median package, the mean, and the highest package separately, and state how many students were placed.

Steps to Choose a Measure of Centre
  1. Look at the distribution first
    Histogram or describe(); is it skewed or are there outliers?
  2. Symmetric, no outliers
    Report the mean (with standard deviation).
  3. Skewed or outliers
    Report the median (with IQR), optionally also the mean.
  4. Categories
    Report the mode and the share of each category.
  5. Combining groups
    Use a weighted mean, never the plain mean of group means.
Cross Questions
Mean > median. What does that tell you?
The distribution is probably right-skewed (a long tail of large values) or has high outliers.
Can the median be misleading?
Yes: it ignores how far values are from the middle, so two very different distributions can share a median. Report spread too.
What is the mode of continuous data?
Exact repeated values are rare, so the mode is taken from a histogram bin or a density estimate (the peak).
Why does the average of centre averages differ from the overall average?
Centres have different sizes; each student should count once, so group means must be weighted by group size.

How do you measure spread? Explain range, variance, standard deviation, IQR and coefficient of variation.

Descriptive Statistics 8 min read Difficulty: Beginner 3 of 40
Definition

Range = max − min (uses only two values). Variance = average squared deviation from the mean; standard deviation (SD) = √variance, in the original units. For a sample divide by n − 1 (Bessel's correction, ddof=1), because deviations from the sample mean are slightly too small; pandas uses ddof=1, NumPy defaults to ddof=0.

IQR = Q3 − Q1, the spread of the middle 50 %, robust to outliers (pair it with the median). MAD (median absolute deviation) is another robust measure. Coefficient of variation CV = SD ÷ mean compares variability between variables with different units or scales (ratio data only).

range variance standard deviation ddof IQR MAD coefficient of variation
Analogy
Two batches both average 60 marks. In one everyone scored 55–65; in the other half scored 30 and half 90. The average hides the difference; the spread reveals it, like two cricketers with the same average where one is consistent and the other is all-or-nothing.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Two batches with the same mean of 60 can have completely different spreads.
  2. 2Spread starts with deviations from the mean.
  3. 3Square and average them for the variance; dividing by n − 1 for a sample gives the unbiased estimate. SD brings it back to marks.
  4. 4IQR is robust to outliers; CV compares variability across different scales.
  5. 5Pair each centre measure with its spread measure and watch the ddof default.
Real-World Scenario
Consistency across centres

Two centres have similar mean final scores, but one has a much larger standard deviation: some students are being left behind. The coordinator compares SD and IQR per centre, not just averages, before deciding where to add remedial sessions.

Cross Questions
Why square the deviations instead of using absolute values?
Squares are mathematically convenient (differentiable, variances of independent variables add) and connect to the normal distribution; absolute deviations give the more robust MAD.
Why n − 1?
The sample mean is computed from the same data, so deviations around it are systematically smaller than around the true mean; dividing by n − 1 makes the variance estimate unbiased.
When is CV meaningless?
For interval data or means near zero (e.g. temperature in °C, profit that can be negative): dividing by a mean close to 0 explodes.
SD is 0. What does that mean?
Every value is identical; the column carries no information.

What are percentiles and quartiles? How does a box plot detect outliers with the 1.5 × IQR rule?

Descriptive Statistics 8 min read Difficulty: Beginner 4 of 40
Definition

The p-th percentile is the value below which p % of the data falls. Quartiles are the 25th (Q1), 50th (Q2 = median) and 75th (Q3) percentiles. The five-number summary (min, Q1, median, Q3, max) is what a box plot draws: the box spans Q1–Q3, the line is the median, the whiskers reach the furthest points within the fences.

Tukey's fences: values below Q1 − 1.5·IQR or above Q3 + 1.5·IQR are flagged as outliers (3·IQR for "extreme"). A percentile rank answers the opposite question: "what percentage scored below this student?". Different software uses slightly different interpolation rules for percentiles on small samples.

percentile quartile five-number summary box plot IQR fences percentile rank
Analogy
Line up the whole batch by height. The quartiles are the people standing at the quarter, half and three-quarter marks of the line; anyone standing far beyond the end of the line (past the fences) is the one you look at twice.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Sort the data and find the quartiles: 25 % of packages are below 4.7 LPA, half below 6.0, 75 % below 7.9.
  2. 2The box spans Q1 to Q3 (the middle 50 %, IQR = 3.2); the line inside is the median.
  3. 3Tukey's fences sit 1.5 × IQR beyond the box.
  4. 4Whiskers reach the most extreme points inside the fences; points beyond are drawn individually as outliers.
  5. 5The same percentile machinery gives thresholds (top 10 %) and percentile ranks for individual students.
Real-World Scenario
"Top 10 %" and data checks

Merit scholarships go to students above the 90th percentile of the final score, and each student's report shows their percentile rank. The same fences flag suspicious package entries (a 19.8 LPA package might be real, or a typo) for checking before the placement report is published.

Steps to Use Percentiles and Box Plots
  1. Compute quartiles
    Series.quantile([.25, .5, .75]) or np.percentile.
  2. Compute IQR and fences
    Q1 − 1.5·IQR, Q3 + 1.5·IQR.
  3. Flag, do not delete

Cross Questions
Are all box-plot outliers errors?
No. In skewed data (packages, incomes) the long tail naturally exceeds the fences; they must be checked, not removed automatically.

Descriptive Statistics 7 min read Difficulty: Beginner 5 of 40
Definition

Skewness measures asymmetry: 0 for symmetric data, positive (right-skewed) when a long tail extends to large values (mean > median: income, study hours), negative (left-skewed) when the tail goes to small values (an easy exam: most score high, a few very low). What are the basic rules of probability? Explain mutually exclusive and independent events.

Probability 8 min read Difficulty: Basic 6 of 40
Definition

Probability P(A) is a number between 0 and 1: the long-run share of trials in which event A happens. The complement rule: P(not A) = 1 − P(A). The addition rule: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B); for mutually exclusive events (cannot happen together) P(A and B) = 0.

Independent events do not affect each other: P(A and B) = P(A) · P(B), equivalently P(A | B) = P(A). Mutually exclusive is not the same as independent: if A and B cannot occur together, knowing B happened tells you A did not, so they are strongly dependent.

Analogy
Drawing a card: "heart" and "spade" are mutually exclusive (one card cannot be both). "Heart" and "king" are independent: being a heart does not change the 1-in-13 chance of being a king.

What is conditional probability? Explain Bayes' theorem with an example.

Probability 9 min read Difficulty: Basic 7 of 40
Definition

Conditional probability P(A | B) = P(A and B) ÷ P(B): the probability of A given that B happened, i.e. within the rows where B is true. In general P(A | B) ≠ P(B | A).

Bayes' theorem reverses the condition: P(A | B) = P(B | A) · P(A) ÷ P(B), with P(B) = P(B | A)P(A) + P(B | not A)P(not A). P(A) is the prior (base rate), P(B | A) the likelihood, P(A | B) the posterior. Ignoring the base rate (the base-rate fallacy) is the classic mistake: a very accurate test for a rare condition still produces mostly false alarms.

conditional probability Bayes theorem prior likelihood posterior base-rate fallacy
Analogy
A smoke alarm almost always rings during a fire, but it also rings when you burn toast. Since toast is burnt far more often than houses catch fire, a ringing alarm most likely means toast. Bayes' theorem puts numbers on that intuition.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5

  2. 2The tool flags 95 % of copied reports: 285.
  3. 3It also flags 4 % of honest reports: 388 false alarms.
  4. 4Among all flagged reports only 285 of 673 are copied: Bayes' theorem gives 0.423.
  5. 5A second independent check updates the prior again; the simulation confirms the formula.

What is a random variable? How do you compute expected value and variance?

Probability 8 min read Difficulty: Basic 8 of 40
Definition

A random variable X assigns a number to each outcome of a random process. Discrete random variables have a probability mass function (P(X = x) for each value); continuous ones have a probability density function, where probabilities are areas under the curve.

Expected value E[X] = Σ x · P(X = x): the long-run average. Variance Var(X) = E[(X − E[X])²] = E[X²] − E[X]². Rules: E[aX + b] = aE[X] + b, Var(aX + b) = a²Var(X), E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y] always, and Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) when X and Y are independent.

How do permutations and combinations help count outcomes? Solve the birthday problem.

Probability 8 min read Difficulty: Basic 9 of 40
Definition

Multiplication principle: k independent choices with n₁, n₂, … options give n₁·n₂·… outcomes. Permutations (order matters): choosing r of n in order = n! ÷ (n − r)!. Combinations (order does not matter): C(n, r) = n! ÷ (r!(n − r)!), i.e. permutations divided by the r! orderings of each group.

With equally likely outcomes, probability = favourable ÷ total, so counting is often the whole problem. Python: math.perm, math.comb, math.factorial, and itertools.permutations / combinations to list the outcomes themselves.

What is the law of large numbers? How does Monte Carlo simulation estimate probabilities?

Probability 8 min read Difficulty: Basic 10 of 40
Definition

The law of large numbers (LLN): as the number of independent trials grows, the sample average converges to the expected value (and an observed proportion to the true probability). It says nothing about short runs: after five heads, the next toss is still 50/50 (the gambler's fallacy).

Monte Carlo simulation uses this: generate many random scenarios with a random number generator, compute the quantity of interest each time, and average. The error shrinks like 1/√n, so 100× more simulations give 10× more precision. Fix the seed (np.random.default_rng(seed)) for reproducible results.

law of large numbers Monte Carlo simulation random seed gambler's fallacy 1/√n
Analogy
Toss a coin 10 times and you might get 7 heads; toss it 10,000 times and you will be very close to half. Noise averages out, which is why insurers can predict total claims while no single policyholder is predictable.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Each roll of a fair die has expected value 3.5.
  2. 2The running average wanders early but settles towards 3.5 as rolls accumulate.
  3. 3Convergence is a trend, not a promise for every step; the gambler's fallacy misreads the law.
  4. 4Monte Carlo: simulate many days of attendance to estimate a probability; it matches the exact binomial answer.
  5. 5Precision grows only with the square root of the number of simulations.

After 5 heads in a row, is tails "due"?
No. Independent tosses have no memory; the LLN works by swamping early deviations with many new tosses, not by correcting them.
Why does the error shrink only like 1/√n?
The standard error of a mean is σ/√n; halving the error needs four times as many simulations.

When the system is complex (many interacting random parts, business rules) or no closed-form answer exists; also to check formulas.

Explain the binomial and Poisson distributions. When do you use each?

Probability Distributions 8 min read Difficulty: Intermediate 11 of 40
Definition

Binomial(n, p): the number of successes in n independent trials, each with success probability p (placed / not placed, defective / fine). P(X = k) = C(n, k)·pᵏ·(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ; mean np, variance np(1 − p).

Poisson(λ): the number of events in a fixed interval when events occur independently at a constant average rate λ (doubts raised per hour, server errors per day, typos per page). P(X = k) = e^(−λ)·λᵏ / k!; mean = variance = λ. For large n and small p, Binomial(n, p) ≈ Poisson(np). In SciPy: stats.binom, stats.poisson with pmf, cdf, sf (= 1 − cdf), ppf, rvs.

binomial Poisson pmf cdf sf Bernoulli trial rate λ
Analogy

Step 1 / 5

  5. 5For many trials with a small p, the binomial is almost identical to a Poisson with λ = np.

What is the normal distribution? Explain z-scores and the 68-95-99.7 rule.

Probability Distributions 9 min read Difficulty: Intermediate 12 of 40
Definition

The normal (Gaussian) distribution N(μ, σ) is the symmetric bell curve fully described by its mean μ and standard deviation σ. Many measurements are approximately normal because they add up many small independent effects, and sample means are approximately normal thanks to the central limit theorem.

A z-score z = (x − μ) / σ says how many SDs a value is from the mean, putting different scales on one footing. The empirical rule: about 68 % of values lie within ±1σ, 95 % within ±2σ (exactly ±1.96σ), 99.7 % within ±3σ. stats.norm.cdf(z) gives the area to the left; stats.norm.ppf(q) the cut-off for a given area.

normal distribution Gaussian z-score standardisation empirical rule cdf / ppf
Analogy
A z-score is a common currency: 75 marks in an easy paper and 62 in a hard one can only be compared after converting both to "how far above the class average, in SDs".

Step 1 / 5

  4. 4A z-score locates a value: 75 marks is 1.56 SDs above the mean, beating about 94 % of students.

What is the difference between a PDF and a CDF? Explain the uniform and exponential distributions.

Probability Distributions 8 min read Difficulty: Intermediate 13 of 40
Definition

For a continuous variable, the PDF (density) f(x) is the height of the curve; probabilities are areas: P(a < X < b) = ∫ f(x) dx. Density values can exceed 1. The CDF F(x) = P(X ≤ x) rises from 0 to 1; P(a < X < b) = F(b) − F(a). The inverse CDF (ppf) gives quantiles.

Uniform(a, b): every value in [a, b] equally likely; mean (a + b)/2. Exponential(λ): waiting time until the next event in a Poisson process; mean 1/λ, P(T > t) = e^(−λt), and it is memoryless: having waited 5 minutes does not change the distribution of the remaining wait. SciPy uses scale = 1/λ.

PDF CDF ppf uniform exponential memoryless scale parameter
Analogy
The PDF is a map of crowd density along a street; the CDF is the running count of people you have passed so far. You find how many people are between two shops by subtracting two running counts.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1The exponential PDF describes waiting times; its height is a density, not a probability.
  2. 2Probabilities are areas: a gap longer than one minute happens about 5 % of the time.
  3. 3The CDF accumulates the area from the left and rises from 0 to 1.
  4. 4Read the CDF backwards (ppf) to get the median or a 90th-percentile timeout; differences of the CDF give interval probabilities.
  5. 5The exponential forgets how long you have waited, and exponential gaps produce Poisson counts.

What are the t, chi-square and F distributions, and where are they used?

Probability Distributions 8 min read Difficulty: Intermediate 14 of 40
Definition

These sampling distributions describe test statistics computed from normal samples. The t distribution (degrees of freedom df = n − 1 for one mean) looks like a normal with heavier tails, because σ is estimated from the sample; as df grows it approaches N(0, 1). Used in t-tests and confidence intervals for means and regression coefficients.

The chi-square distribution (sum of squared standard normals; right-skewed, mean = df) is used for variance tests, goodness-of-fit and contingency-table tests. The F distribution (ratio of two scaled chi-squares) compares variances: ANOVA and the overall F-test in regression.

t distribution degrees of freedom chi-square F distribution critical value sampling distribution
Analogy
The t distribution is the normal curve wearing a safety margin: with only a few observations you are less sure of σ, so the tails are fatter and the critical values wider. With many observations the margin is no longer needed.

Step 1 / 5
  1. 1The standard normal curve, used when σ is known.
  2. 2With σ estimated from very few observations, the statistic follows a t distribution with much fatter tails.

  4. 4Using 1.96 with n = 6 would produce "significant" results twice as often as intended.
  5. 5Chi-square and F are the sampling distributions behind variance, contingency-table and ANOVA tests.

How do you check whether data is normally distributed? Explain Q-Q plots and the Shapiro-Wilk test.

Probability Distributions 8 min read Difficulty: Intermediate 15 of 40
Definition

Visual checks come first: a histogram and a Q-Q plot, which plots sample quantiles against theoretical normal quantiles; normal data falls on a straight line, an S-shape means heavy or light tails, a curve means skew. stats.probplot computes the points and the correlation of the line.

Formal tests: Shapiro-Wilk (stats.shapiro, powerful for small and medium n), D'Agostino-Pearson (stats.normaltest, based on skew and kurtosis), Anderson-Darling, Kolmogorov-Smirnov. H₀ = "the data is normal"; a small p-value rejects normality. With large samples, tests reject even trivial deviations; with small samples, they miss real ones — so combine tests with plots and the question "does the deviation matter for my method?".

Analogy
A Q-Q plot is a height chart against the "ideal" class: line up your students and an imaginary perfectly normal class by rank. If each of your students is about as tall as the matching ideal student, the points form a straight line.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Plot each sorted value against the quantile a normal distribution would predict. Right-skewed packages bend upwards.
  2. 2After a log transform, the points lie close to the straight line.
  3. 3The Q-Q correlation and Shapiro-Wilk test summarise the same idea: log packages pass, raw packages fail.
  4. 4The same slightly non-normal data is rarely rejected at n = 20 and always rejected at n = 5,000.

What is the difference between a population and a sample? Explain sampling methods and sampling bias.

Sampling & Estimation 8 min read Difficulty: Intermediate 16 of 40
Definition

The population is everyone you want to draw conclusions about; a sample is the subset you actually measure. Numbers describing the population are parameters (μ, σ, p); numbers computed from a sample are statistics (x̄, s, p̂) that estimate them.

Probability sampling: simple random (every unit equally likely), stratified (random samples within groups such as centres, guaranteeing each is represented), cluster (randomly pick whole groups, e.g. batches, then survey everyone in them), systematic (every k-th unit). Convenience and voluntary-response samples are cheap but biased. Sampling bias (who gets in) cannot be fixed by a larger sample.

population sample parameter statistic stratified cluster sampling bias
Analogy
To check whether a pot of soup is salty you stir it and taste a spoonful. A random sample is the stirred spoon; tasting only the top layer is a biased sample, and a bigger spoon from the top layer does not help.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Simple random sampling: every student has the same chance; centres may be over- or under-represented by luck.
  2. 2Stratified sampling: a random sample inside each centre guarantees every group is represented.
  3. 3Cluster sampling takes whole groups (a batch); systematic sampling takes every k-th student from a list.
  4. 4A convenience sample of the most regular attenders overstates both scores and placement.
  5. 5Larger random samples get closer to the truth; larger biased samples stay biased.
Real-World Scenario
Course feedback survey

Asking only students who stay back after class (convenience) over-represents the keen ones. A stratified random sample of 20 students per centre gives every centre a voice and smaller error for the same effort, and answers can be weighted back to the real centre sizes.

What is the central limit theorem and why is it so important?

Sampling & Estimation 9 min read Difficulty: Intermediate 17 of 40
Definition

The central limit theorem (CLT): for independent samples of size n from any population with mean μ and finite SD σ, the distribution of the sample mean approaches a normal distribution N(μ, σ/√n) as n grows, whatever the shape of the population. For moderately skewed data n ≈ 30 is often enough; strongly skewed data needs more.

Why it matters: it justifies normal-based confidence intervals and t/z-tests for means and proportions even when the raw data is not normal, and it explains why averages are much more stable than individual values (spread shrinks by √n).

central limit theorem sampling distribution sample mean σ/√n normal approximation
Analogy
One student's package is unpredictable. The average package of a random group of 50 is much more predictable, and if you repeat the experiment many times, those averages pile up into a neat bell curve, even though individual packages are very skewed.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Individual packages are strongly right-skewed.
  2. 2Averages of 5 random packages are already less skewed and much narrower.
  3. 3Averages of 30 look almost normal.
  4. 4With 100 the bell is narrow and symmetric; its centre stays at μ and its SD matches σ/√n.
  5. 5This is why intervals and tests for means work even when the raw data is not normal.
Real-World Scenario
Why batch averages are trustworthy

Individual placement packages are strongly right-skewed, but the mean package of a batch of 50 placed students behaves almost normally. That is what lets the placement cell attach a margin of error to "average package this year" and compare it with last year using a t-test.

What is the standard error? How is it different from the standard deviation?

Sampling & Estimation 7 min read Difficulty: Intermediate 18 of 40
Definition

The standard deviation describes how much individual values vary. The standard error (SE) describes how much a statistic (such as the sample mean) would vary from sample to sample: SE(x̄) = s/√n. For a proportion, SE(p̂) = √(p̂(1 − p̂)/n).

SD does not shrink with more data; SE does, by √n: quadrupling the sample halves the SE. SE is the building block of confidence intervals (estimate ± critical value × SE) and test statistics (difference ÷ SE). scipy.stats.sem computes s/√n (ddof=1).

standard error standard deviation sem s/√n precision sample size
Analogy
SD is how much individual archers' arrows scatter; SE is how much the average point of a team's arrows moves from one round to the next. A bigger team does not make each archer steadier, but its average becomes much steadier.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1The SD describes how much individual students differ: about ±4.3 hours around the mean.
  2. 2The SE describes how much the mean of a sample of 25 would move between samples.
  3. 3With 100 students the SE halves.
  4. 4With 400 it halves again: SE shrinks with √n while the SD stays put.
  5. 5The same idea gives the SE of a proportion and tells you the sample size needed for a target precision.
Real-World Scenario
Reporting a survey result

"Average weekly study hours: 8.6 ± 0.18 (SE), n = 600" tells the reader the estimate is precise; "SD 4.3" tells them students differ a lot. Confusing the two (reporting mean ± SE as if it were the spread of students) makes data look far more consistent than it is.

What is a confidence interval? How do you compute and interpret one?

Sampling & Estimation 9 min read Difficulty: Intermediate 19 of 40
Definition

A confidence interval (CI) gives a range of plausible values for a parameter: estimate ± critical value × SE. For a mean with unknown σ: x̄ ± t(0.975, n − 1)·s/√n. For a proportion, the Wilson interval is better than the simple p̂ ± 1.96·SE, especially for small n or p near 0 or 1.

Interpretation: a 95 % CI comes from a procedure that captures the true value in 95 % of repeated samples. It is not "a 95 % probability that μ is in this particular interval" (μ is fixed). Higher confidence → wider interval; larger n → narrower (∝ 1/√n).

confidence interval margin of error t interval Wilson interval coverage confidence level
Analogy
A CI is a fishing net thrown at a fish you cannot see. The net is designed so that 95 out of 100 throws catch the fish. For any single throw, the fish is either in the net or not; the 95 % describes the net, not the fish.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Each sample gives its own interval around its own mean.
  2. 2Over many samples, about 95 % of the intervals cover the true mean; a few miss.
  3. 3For the real final scores: estimate ± t × SE. Higher confidence means a wider interval.
  4. 4For proportions, Wilson behaves well even for small samples where the simple Wald interval breaks down.
  5. 5Simulation confirms the coverage: the 95 % belongs to the method, not to a single interval.

What is bootstrapping? How do you get a confidence interval for a median?

Sampling & Estimation 8 min read Difficulty: Intermediate 20 of 40
Definition

The bootstrap estimates the sampling distribution of any statistic by resampling the data with replacement: draw n rows from the n observed rows many times (e.g. 10,000), compute the statistic each time, and look at the spread of those values. The SD of the bootstrap statistics estimates the SE; the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles give a percentile CI.

It needs no formula and no normality assumption, so it works for medians, percentiles, ratios, correlations and model metrics. scipy.stats.bootstrap does this for you and also offers the BCa interval (more accurate for smooth statistics such as means; it can fail for medians of data with many tied values). Limits: it assumes the sample represents the population, struggles with very small samples and extreme statistics (max), and must resample whole units (students, not rows) when data is grouped.

bootstrap resampling with replacement percentile interval BCa scipy.stats.bootstrap
Analogy
You cannot survey the town again, so you photocopy your sample and draw new "samples" from the copies. How much the answer changes between these pretend surveys shows how much it would change between real ones.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Start from the one sample you have.
  2. 2Draw new samples of the same size with replacement: some students appear twice, others not at all.
  3. 3Compute the statistic (the median) for every resample.
  4. 4The pile of bootstrap medians shows how much the median would vary between samples.
  5. 5Its 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles form a 95 % confidence interval, with no formula needed.
Real-World Scenario

Explain hypothesis testing: null and alternative hypotheses, test statistic, p-value and significance level.

Hypothesis Testing 10 min read Difficulty: Advanced 21 of 40
Definition

A hypothesis test asks whether the data is surprising if a default claim is true. The null hypothesis H₀ is the "no effect / no difference" claim (mean score = 60); the alternative H₁ is what you want evidence for (mean ≠ 60). A test statistic measures how far the data is from H₀ in standard-error units, e.g. t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s/√n).

The p-value is the probability, assuming H₀ is true, of getting a result at least as extreme as the one observed. If p ≤ the significance level α (chosen beforehand, usually 0.05), reject H₀; otherwise "fail to reject" (not "accept"). The p-value is not the probability that H₀ is true, and a small p-value says nothing about the size or importance of the effect.

null hypothesis alternative hypothesis test statistic p-value significance level α one-sample t-test
Analogy
A court trial: the defendant is presumed innocent (H₀). The prosecution must show evidence that would be very unlikely if he were innocent (small p-value). "Not guilty" means the evidence was insufficient, not that innocence was proven.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Assume H₀ is true (mean = 60): t would follow this distribution.
  2. 2Compute the test statistic from the data: the mean is 3.0 standard errors below 60.
  3. 3The p-value is the area beyond the observed statistic in both tails: 0.28 %. A simulation of null batches gives the same answer.
  4. 4With α = 0.05 the cut-offs are ±1.964; the observed t is beyond them, so H₀ is rejected.
  5. 5A small sample with the same drop is not significant: absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.
Real-World Scenario

What are Type I and Type II errors? What is statistical power and how do you choose a sample size?

Hypothesis Testing 9 min read Difficulty: Advanced 22 of 40
Definition

Type I error (false positive): rejecting a true H₀; its probability is α. Type II error (false negative): failing to reject a false H₀; its probability is β. Power = 1 − β = the probability of detecting an effect that really exists.

Power grows with a larger sample size, a larger effect size (difference ÷ SD, Cohen's d), a larger α, and lower noise. Before a study, a power analysis fixes three of (α, power, effect size, n) and solves for the fourth; the usual target is 80 % power at α = 0.05. statsmodels: TTestIndPower().solve_power, NormalIndPower for proportions.

Type I error Type II error α β power effect size sample size Cohen's d
Analogy
A fire alarm: a Type I error rings with no fire (annoying), a Type II error stays silent during a fire (dangerous). Making the alarm less sensitive reduces false alarms but increases missed fires; only a better detector (more data) reduces both.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1If H₀ is true, the test statistic follows the grey curve; we reject beyond the critical value.
  2. 2The red tail is the Type I error rate α: rejecting although nothing is going on.
  3. 3If there is a real effect, the statistic follows the green curve. The part left of the critical value is β, a missed effect (Type II error).
  4. 4The rest is power. For a 3-mark effect, about 250 students per group give 80 % power.
  5. 5A power analysis solves for the sample size before the study starts.

How does an independent two-sample t-test work? What is Welch's t-test and Cohen's d?

Hypothesis Testing 9 min read Difficulty: Advanced 23 of 40
Definition

The independent two-sample t-test compares the means of two separate groups: t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / SE(difference). Assumptions: independent observations, roughly normal sample means (fine for moderate n), and for Student's version equal variances.

Welch's t-test (equal_var=False) does not assume equal variances and adjusts the degrees of freedom; it loses almost nothing when variances are equal, so it is the safer default. Report the difference with a confidence interval and an effect size such as Cohen's d = difference ÷ pooled SD (0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large).

ttest_ind Welch equal_var Levene test Cohen's d difference in means
Analogy
Comparing two classes by their averages is like comparing two archers by where their arrows centre: the further apart the centres and the tighter each group of arrows, the more convincing the difference.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1The two groups' post-test scores overlap heavily.
  2. 2Their means differ by 3.45 marks.
  3. 3The t statistic divides that difference by its standard error. Variances are similar (Levene p = 0.47), so Student and Welch agree; Welch is the safe default.
  4. 4Report the effect: the difference with a 95 % CI and Cohen's d.
  5. 5An effect on one outcome can be invisible on a noisier downstream outcome.

When do you use a paired t-test? Why is it more powerful than an independent test for before/after data?

Hypothesis Testing 8 min read Difficulty: Advanced 24 of 40
Definition

A paired t-test compares two measurements on the same units: before/after, left/right, two raters on the same scripts. It computes each unit's difference dᵢ and runs a one-sample t-test on the differences: t = d̄ / (s_d/√n). stats.ttest_rel(after, before).

Because each student is compared with themselves, stable differences between students (some are always strong) cancel out, so the SD of the differences is much smaller than the SD of the scores and the test is far more powerful. Treating paired data as two independent samples is a classic mistake; it wastes this information (and violates independence).

ttest_rel paired samples before/after differences repeated measures
Analogy
To test whether new running shoes are faster, time the same runners in old and new shoes. Comparing a group of runners in old shoes with a different group in new shoes mixes the shoe effect with who happens to be fast.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Every student has a pre-test and a post-test score.
  2. 2The paired test works on each student's difference, removing the large differences between students.
  3. 3Pre and post scores are strongly correlated, so the SD of the differences (7.47) is much smaller than the SD of the scores.
  4. 4Treating the data as two independent groups doubles the SE and wastes that information.
  5. 5The advantage is largest for small samples. Report the mean gain with its CI.

One-tailed vs two-tailed tests: when are they appropriate? How do you test a proportion with a z-test?

Hypothesis Testing 8 min read Difficulty: Advanced 25 of 40
Definition

A two-tailed test (H₁: μ ≠ μ₀) looks for a difference in either direction and splits α between both tails. A one-tailed test (H₁: μ > μ₀ or μ < μ₀) puts all of α in one tail, so it detects effects in that direction more easily but can never detect the opposite. It is justified only when the direction is fixed before seeing the data and an effect the other way would lead to the same action as no effect.

For proportions with large samples, the z-test uses z = (p̂ − p₀) / √(p₀(1 − p₀)/n). statsmodels: proportions_ztest(count, nobs, value, alternative=...) for one or two proportions; binomtest (SciPy) for the exact one-sample version with small samples.

two-tailed one-tailed alternative= proportions_ztest binomtest z-test
Analogy
A two-tailed test is a goalkeeper guarding both posts; a one-tailed test moves to cover one post better and leaves the other one open. You only do that if you know the shot can only come from one side.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1A two-tailed test splits α between both tails: it can detect a change in either direction.
  2. 2A one-tailed test puts all of α on one side, so its cut-off is closer to 0 (1.645).
  3. 3The placement rate is 2.33 standard errors below the 40 % target; the one-sided p is half the two-sided one.
  4. 4Testing the wrong direction makes the real effect invisible.
  5. 5Proportion tests compare a rate with a target or two rates with each other.

How do chi-square tests work? Explain goodness of fit and the test of independence.

More Statistical Tests 9 min read Difficulty: Advanced 26 of 40
Definition

Chi-square tests compare observed counts O with expected counts E: χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E. Large values mean the counts are far from what H₀ predicts. Goodness of fit (stats.chisquare): does one categorical variable follow a given distribution (e.g. the planned course mix)? df = categories − 1.

Test of independence (stats.chi2_contingency): are two categorical variables related (centre × placed)? Expected count for a cell = row total × column total ÷ grand total; df = (rows − 1)(cols − 1). Rule of thumb: expected counts ≥ 5 (otherwise Fisher's exact test). Effect size: Cramér's V (0 = none, 1 = perfect association).

chi-square goodness of fit contingency table chi2_contingency expected counts Cramér's V Fisher exact
Analogy
If placement had nothing to do with centre, each centre would get its "fair share" of placements in proportion to its size. The chi-square statistic adds up how unfair the actual shares are, cell by cell.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Start from the table of observed counts.
  2. 2If centre and placement were independent, each centre would get placements in proportion to its size: the expected counts.
  3. 3The chi-square statistic sums the squared gaps relative to the expected counts; with 2 degrees of freedom p = 0.009.
  4. 4Standardised residuals show where the gap is: Kharghar has more placements than expected.
  5. 5Report the strength (Cramér's V) too: the association is real but weak.

    One variable vs expected shares → chisquare; two variables → chi2_contingency.
  3. Check expected counts
    All ≥ 5; otherwise merge categories or use Fisher's exact test.
  4. Read the result
    p-value; then residuals to see which cells differ.
  5. Report the strength

What is ANOVA? How do you compare more than two group means and find which groups differ?

More Statistical Tests 10 min read Difficulty: Advanced 27 of 40
Definition

One-way ANOVA tests H₀: all group means are equal, against "at least one differs". It compares variation between group means with variation within groups: F = MS_between / MS_within. A large F means the group means are further apart than within-group noise would explain. stats.f_oneway(*groups); statsmodels anova_lm for the full table.

Running many pairwise t-tests instead inflates false positives, so ANOVA comes first and a post-hoc test (Tukey HSD: stats.tukey_hsd or statsmodels pairwise_tukeyhsd) then finds which pairs differ while controlling the family-wise error rate. Assumptions: independence, roughly normal residuals, similar variances (Welch ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis otherwise). Effect size: η² = SS_between / SS_total.

ANOVA F statistic f_oneway Tukey HSD post-hoc eta squared between / within variance
Analogy
Three cooks make the same dish. ANOVA asks whether the differences between cooks are larger than the natural variation in each cook's own dishes. Only if they are does it make sense to ask which cook is different.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Final scores of students in three centres.
  2. 2Between-group variation: how far the centre means are from the grand mean.
  3. 3Within-group variation: how much students differ inside each centre.
  4. 4F compares the two. Here the centre means are almost identical relative to the spread inside centres, so F is small and η² tiny.
  5. 5Only a significant ANOVA is followed by post-hoc comparisons; ANOVA is a special case of regression.

What are non-parametric tests? When would you use Mann-Whitney, Wilcoxon and Kruskal-Wallis?

More Statistical Tests 8 min read Difficulty: Advanced 28 of 40
Definition

Non-parametric (rank-based) tests make fewer assumptions about the distribution. They replace values by their ranks, so they are robust to outliers and skew and work for ordinal data. The price: slightly less power when the data really is normal, and they test "one group tends to have larger values" rather than "means differ".

Equivalents: Mann-Whitney U (mannwhitneyu) for two independent groups (vs independent t-test); Wilcoxon signed-rank (wilcoxon) for paired data (vs paired t-test); Kruskal-Wallis H (kruskal) for 3+ groups (vs one-way ANOVA); Spearman correlation (vs Pearson).

non-parametric ranks Mann-Whitney U Wilcoxon signed-rank Kruskal-Wallis robust
Analogy
Instead of comparing the exact finishing times of two running clubs (one runner with a broken leg could ruin an average), compare their finishing positions. Ranks do not care how far behind the last runner was.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Start from the raw values, including an extreme one.
  2. 2Replace every value by its rank: the extreme value becomes just the largest rank.
  3. 3Each common parametric test has a rank-based counterpart.
  4. 4A single 60 LPA offer in the other group wipes out the t-test result (p 0.014 → 0.45); the rank test barely moves.
  5. 5Rank tests suit skewed data, outliers and ordinal scales; report medians with them.

What is the multiple-testing problem? Explain Bonferroni, Holm and false-discovery-rate corrections.

More Statistical Tests 8 min read Difficulty: Advanced 29 of 40
Definition

Each test at α = 0.05 has a 5 % false-positive rate. Running m independent tests on pure noise gives P(at least one false positive) = 1 − 0.95ᵐ (64 % for 20 tests): the family-wise error rate (FWER) explodes. Testing many subgroups, metrics or outcomes and reporting the significant ones is a form of p-hacking.

Corrections: Bonferroni (test each at α/m; simple, conservative), Holm (step-down, controls FWER, always at least as powerful as Bonferroni), and Benjamini-Hochberg (controls the false discovery rate, the expected share of false positives among discoveries; preferred when screening many hypotheses). statsmodels: multipletests(p, method=...).

multiple comparisons family-wise error rate Bonferroni Holm Benjamini-Hochberg FDR p-hacking
Analogy
Buy one lottery ticket and winning is a surprise. Buy twenty tickets and one small win is expected. Testing twenty hypotheses is buying twenty tickets: a "significant" result is no longer surprising.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1With α = 0.05, each test on pure noise has a 1-in-20 chance of looking significant.
  2. 2The chance of at least one false positive grows quickly with the number of tests.
  3. 3A simulation of 1,000 analysts confirms it: almost two thirds "discover" something in noise.
  4. 4Corrections raise the bar: Bonferroni and Holm control the family-wise error rate, Benjamini-Hochberg the false discovery rate.
  5. 5The price is power; pre-registering a primary hypothesis avoids the problem.
Real-World Scenario
"Which factors affect placement?"

An analyst tests placement against 20 recorded attributes (hostel, bus route, birth month, …) and finds two with p < 0.05. With 20 tests, about one false positive is expected anyway; after a Holm or BH correction neither survives, and birth month is quietly dropped from the report.

Cross Questions
Why is Bonferroni conservative?
It assumes the worst case; with many tests it requires tiny p-values and misses real effects (low power). Holm is uniformly better.
FWER vs FDR?
FWER: probability of any false positive. FDR: expected proportion of false positives among the rejected hypotheses, which allows a few errors in exchange for more discoveries.
Is looking at the data repeatedly during an A/B test also multiple testing?
Yes. Peeking and stopping as soon as p < 0.05 inflates false positives; use a fixed sample size or sequential testing methods.

Statistical significance vs practical significance: why should you report effect sizes and confidence intervals?

More Statistical Tests 8 min read Difficulty: Advanced 30 of 40
Definition

A p-value mixes two things: the size of the effect and the sample size. With a huge sample, trivial differences become "highly significant"; with a small one, important differences may not reach significance. A p-value alone therefore cannot say whether an effect matters.

Always report an effect size in meaningful units (difference in marks, difference in placement rate, odds ratio) with its confidence interval, plus a standardised effect where helpful: Cohen's d, r, η², Cramér's V. Decide beforehand what the smallest effect of practical interest is.

effect size practical significance Cohen's d confidence interval sample size minimum important difference
Analogy
A bathroom scale so precise that it detects you are 2 grams heavier after a glass of water gives a "significant" change. Nobody would call it weight gain. Precision (sample size) is not importance (effect size).
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Decide first which difference would matter in practice: here 2 marks.
  2. 2With 100,000 students per group, a 0.4-mark difference is extremely "significant" but the whole CI lies far below 2 marks: unimportant.
  3. 3With 20 per group, a large difference is "not significant": the CI is wide and includes both 0 and big effects. Inconclusive, not "no effect".
  4. 4With 200 per group, the same large effect is estimated precisely and clearly exceeds the important threshold.
  5. 5p-values bounce around between replications; effect sizes with CIs carry the real information.
Real-World Scenario
A national comparison

Across 200,000 students nationwide, students who submit assignments online score 0.4 marks higher (p < 0.0001). The effect is real but tiny (d ≈ 0.04); it does not justify an expensive policy change. Conversely, a 5-mark improvement in a 20-student pilot may be worth a larger trial even with p = 0.12.

What are covariance and correlation? Compare Pearson, Spearman and Kendall correlation.

Correlation & Regression 9 min read Difficulty: Advanced 31 of 40
Definition

Covariance measures whether two variables move together, but its size depends on the units. Pearson's r = cov(X, Y) / (s_X·s_Y) rescales it to −1…+1 and measures the strength of a linear relationship. r² is the share of variance in one variable explained by a straight line on the other.

Spearman's ρ is Pearson on ranks: it measures any monotonic relationship and resists outliers. Kendall's τ counts concordant vs discordant pairs; it is robust and preferred for small samples with ties. r ≈ 0 does not mean "no relationship": a U-shape can have r = 0. Always plot the data. stats.pearsonr / spearmanr / kendalltau also give p-values; df.corr(method=...) gives matrices.

covariance Pearson r Spearman ρ Kendall τ correlation matrix r² monotonic
Analogy
Correlation is how tightly a crowd of points follows an invisible straight road. Pearson needs the road to be straight; Spearman only needs it to keep going uphill, however it curves.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1A linear relationship: Pearson and Spearman agree.
  2. 2A strong but U-shaped relationship has Pearson r of zero: correlation only sees straight lines.
  3. 3A curved but always-increasing relationship: Spearman (ranks) is a perfect 1, Pearson is lower.
  4. 4Thirty unrelated points plus one extreme point: Pearson jumps to 0.78, Spearman is not fooled.
  5. 5Interpret r² as variance explained, and always look at the scatter plot.

Correlation does not imply causation: what are confounding variables and how do you control for them?

Correlation & Regression 9 min read Difficulty: Advanced 32 of 40
Definition

Two variables can be correlated because X causes Y, Y causes X (reverse causation), both are caused by a third variable (confounder), or by chance (especially when many pairs are examined). A confounder Z influences both X and Y and creates a correlation between them even if X has no effect on Y.

Ways to deal with it: randomised experiments (random assignment breaks the link between X and all confounders), or, with observational data, adjusting for measured confounders: stratification, partial correlation, multiple regression, matching. Adjustment only handles confounders you measured; causal claims from observational data need strong domain arguments.

causation confounder reverse causation spurious correlation partial correlation randomised experiment
Analogy
Ice-cream sales and drowning deaths rise together. Ice cream does not cause drowning; hot weather (the confounder) causes both. Compare days with the same temperature and the link disappears.

Explain simple linear regression: least squares, slope, intercept, R², and confidence vs prediction intervals.

Correlation & Regression 10 min read Difficulty: Advanced 33 of 40
Definition

Simple linear regression models y = β₀ + β₁x + ε. Ordinary least squares (OLS) picks the line minimising the sum of squared residuals: β₁ = cov(x, y) / var(x), β₀ = ȳ − β₁x̄. The slope is the expected change in y per unit of x; the intercept is the prediction at x = 0 (often not meaningful). R² = 1 − SS_res/SS_tot is the share of variance explained; with one predictor it equals r².

statsmodels reports each coefficient's SE, t, p-value and CI. A confidence interval for the mean response (where is the line?) is narrow; a prediction interval for a single new student (where will this student score?) is much wider because it adds individual noise. smf.ols("y ~ x", df).fit(), .summary(), .get_prediction().summary_frame().

OLS least squares slope intercept R² residual prediction interval statsmodels
Analogy
Fitting a line is like hanging a straight rod among nails with springs attached to each nail: the rod settles where the total spring tension (squared distances) is smallest.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Each point is a student: weekly study hours against final score.
  2. 2OLS finds the line with slope cov(x, y)/var(x): each extra weekly hour is worth about 1.6 marks.
  3. 3Residuals are the vertical distances from the points to the line; the line minimises their squared sum.
  4. 4R² is the share of variance the line explains (44 %); the residual SD is the typical prediction error.
  5. 5The CI for the mean at 12 hours is narrow; the prediction interval for one student (purple band) is about ±15 marks.
Real-World Scenario
"How much does one more study hour help?"

Regression of final score on weekly study hours gives a slope in marks per hour, with a confidence interval, that mentors can quote to students. For an individual student planning 12 hours a week, the prediction interval shows the realistic range of outcomes, not just the average.

How do you interpret a multiple regression? Explain coefficients, dummy variables, adjusted R² and the F-test.

Correlation & Regression 10 min read Difficulty: Advanced 34 of 40
Definition

Multiple regression y = β₀ + β₁x₁ + … + βₖxₖ + ε estimates each coefficient holding the other predictors constant. Categorical variables enter as dummy variables (one per level, minus a reference level): C(centre) in a formula; each dummy coefficient is the difference from the reference group.

Adjusted R² penalises extra predictors (plain R² never decreases when you add one). The F-test checks whether the model as a whole beats an intercept-only model; each coefficient's t-test checks that predictor given the others. Compare nested models with an F-test (anova_lm(m_small, m_big)) or AIC/BIC. Coefficients are only comparable in size after standardising.

multiple regression ceteris paribus dummy variables reference level adjusted R² F-test AIC
Analogy
Asking "what is the effect of study hours?" in a multiple regression is like comparing two students who are identical in attendance, test score and centre and differ only in study hours.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Each numeric coefficient is the change in final score per unit, with the other predictors held fixed; the bars are 95 % CIs.
  2. 2Categorical centre enters as dummies relative to the reference level (Juhu): Pune is worth +2.2 marks, Kharghar nothing.
  3. 3The estimates recover the true effects used to generate the data.
  4. 4R² always rises with extra predictors (even noise); adjusted R² and AIC penalise them. The nested F-test confirms the bigger model is better.
  5. 5The raw centre averages hide the Pune effect because other factors and noise mask it.
Real-World Scenario

What are the assumptions of linear regression and how do you check them? Explain multicollinearity and VIF.

Correlation & Regression 10 min read Difficulty: Advanced 35 of 40
Definition

OLS inference relies on (LINE): Linearity of the relationship, Independence of errors, Normality of errors (mainly for small samples), and Equal variance of errors (homoscedasticity). Check with residual-vs-fitted plots, Q-Q plots of residuals, the Breusch-Pagan test (heteroscedasticity), Jarque-Bera (normality), Durbin-Watson (autocorrelation).

Multicollinearity: predictors strongly correlated with each other inflate coefficient SEs and make individual coefficients unstable (even sign-flipping), though predictions stay fine. Measure with the variance inflation factor VIF = 1/(1 − R²ⱼ); VIF above 5–10 is a warning. Influential points are found with leverage and Cook's distance. Remedies: transformations, robust (HC3) standard errors, dropping or combining collinear predictors, regularisation.

LINE assumptions residual plot heteroscedasticity Breusch-Pagan VIF multicollinearity Cook's distance robust SE
Analogy
Multicollinearity is like trying to judge which of two inseparable twins broke the vase: together they clearly did it, but you cannot tell how much each contributed.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Residuals should scatter randomly around zero with constant spread.
  2. 2A funnel means the error variance grows with the fitted value (heteroscedasticity): coefficients are fine, standard errors are not.
  3. 3Correlated predictors inflate each other's variance: adding pre-test makes the post-test coefficient much less precise.
  4. 4Check each LINE assumption with a plot or test.
  5. 5Find influential points, investigate them, and apply the matching remedy.

Definition

An A/B test is a randomised experiment: units are randomly assigned to control (A) or treatment (B), one primary metric is defined in advance, and the sample size is fixed by a power analysis for the smallest lift worth detecting. Randomisation makes the groups comparable, so a difference in outcome can be attributed to the treatment.

The decision uses the CI against the minimum worthwhile lift, not just p < 0.05.

A/B test randomisation primary metric power analysis two-proportion z-test lift sample ratio mismatch
Analogy
An A/B test is a fair coin toss deciding who gets the new medicine: because chance alone decides, any systematic difference between the groups afterwards must come from the medicine.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Fix the metric and the smallest lift worth acting on; the power analysis gives 1,250 submissions per group for 70 % → 75 %.
  2. 2Randomise students to A or B and run until the planned sample is reached.
  3. 3Compare the rates once, after checking that the split came out as planned.
  4. 4Estimate the lift with a confidence interval and compare it with the minimum worthwhile lift.
  5. 5Decide with the interval and the cost of the reminder in mind.

Definition

Simpson's paradox: a trend that appears in every subgroup reverses (or disappears) when the groups are combined. It happens when a third variable (here, course mix) is associated with both the grouping and the outcome, and the groups have very different compositions.

The aggregated comparison answers "which centre has more placed students overall?"; the stratified comparison answers "for a student in a given course, which centre does better?". Which one is right depends on the causal question. A fair overall comparison standardises rates to a common mix (direct standardisation) or uses a regression with the confounder.

Simpson's paradox confounding stratification aggregation standardised rate weighted average
Analogy
A hospital that takes the most severe cases can have a higher overall death rate yet treat every type of patient better than a hospital that only sees mild cases. The overall number reflects who walks in, not only how well they are treated.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1Overall, South places 72 % of students and North only 48 %.
  2. 2Yet within each course, North places a higher share than South.
  3. 3The reason is the course mix: North mostly teaches PG-DAC, which places fewer students; South mostly PG-DBDA.
  4. 4Applying each centre's course rates to the same mix gives a fair comparison: North is better.
  5. 5A regression including course reverses the odds ratio. Which comparison is right depends on the question being asked.

Scenario: how many students must you survey to estimate satisfaction within ±5 percentage points?

Scenario-Based Questions 8 min read Difficulty: Expert 38 of 40
Definition

For a proportion, the margin of error at 95 % confidence is E = 1.96·√(p(1 − p)/n), so the required sample is n = 1.96²·p(1 − p)/E². If p is unknown, use p = 0.5, the worst case (largest variance): n = 385 for ±5 %, about 1,068 for ±3 %.

For a finite population N, apply the correction n_adj = n / (1 + (n − 1)/N): surveying 300 batch-mates needs far fewer than 385 responses. Then inflate for the expected response rate. With stratified sampling, allocate proportionally (or more to small strata that must be reported separately). Precision needs are per subgroup: ±5 % for each centre needs that n in each centre.

margin of error sample size worst case p = 0.5 finite population correction response rate allocation
Analogy
Like deciding how many spoonfuls to taste: the required number depends on how sure you want to be and how precise, not on the size of the pot, unless the pot is small enough that you are tasting a large part of it.
How It Works — Step by Step
Step 1 / 5
  1. 1The required sample grows with the square of the precision: halving the margin needs four times the responses.
  2. 2±5 points at 95 % confidence needs 385 responses from a large population; ±3 points needs 1,068.
  3. 3For small populations, the finite population correction reduces the requirement sharply.
  4. 4Reporting each centre separately needs the sample in each centre, then inflating for the response rate.
  5. 5Simulation confirms the margin; non-response bias needs a different fix (follow-ups, weighting).

A control chart (statistical process control) plots a statistic over time with a centre line and control limits at about ±3 standard errors, computed from a stable baseline period. Points inside the limits are treated as common-cause (normal) variation; a point outside, or a run of points on one side (e.g. 8 in a row below the centre), signals a special cause worth investigating.

For a proportion (share of students attending) use a p-chart: limits p̄ ± 3·√(p̄(1 − p̄)/n), which widen when fewer students are enrolled. Reacting to every dip inside the limits ("tampering") wastes effort and can make the process worse.

Analogy
Your commute takes 30 to 40 minutes on normal days. A 37-minute trip needs no explanation; a 75-minute trip does. Control limits formalise "normal days" so that you only investigate the real anomalies.

Real-World Scenario
Attendance monitoring

The coordinator tracks the share of enrolled students attending each week. A dip to 80 % after weeks of 82–89 % triggers a panic email, but the p-chart shows it is inside the limits. Week 13, at 69 %, is outside: it coincided with a hackathon in another city, a special cause that explains it.

Example: p-chart for Weekly Attendance with a Baseline and Signals
Python (SciPy · statsmodels)
from scipy import stats
rng = np.random.default_rng(14)
enrolled = 180
p_true = np.r_[np.full(12, 0.85), 0.71, np.full(7, 0.85)]            # week 13 has a special cause
attended = rng.binomial(enrolled, p_true)
weeks = pd.DataFrame({'week': np.arange(1, 21), 'attended': attended, 'rate': (attended / enrolled).round(3)})

base = weeks.iloc[:10]                                                  # baseline period
p_bar = base['attended'].sum() / (10 * enrolled)
se = np.sqrt(p_bar * (1 - p_bar) / enrolled)
lcl, ucl = p_bar - 3 * se, p_bar + 3 * se
print(f'centre line {p_bar:.3f}, control limits [{lcl:.3f}, {ucl:.3f}]  (2-sigma warning [{p_bar - 2 * se:.3f}, {p_bar + 2 * se:.3f}])')

weeks['signal'] = np.where(weeks['rate'] < lcl, 'BELOW LCL', np.where(weeks['rate'] > ucl, 'ABOVE UCL', ''))
print(weeks.to_string(index=False))

low = weeks.loc[weeks['signal'] == '', 'rate'].min()
print(f'lowest in-control week: {low:.3f} -> normal variation; investigate only flagged weeks')
print(f'false-alarm rate per point with 3-sigma limits: {2 * stats.norm.sf(3):.4f}')

Steps to Build a Control Chart
  1. Choose the statistic and chart
    Proportion → p-chart; mean → X̄ chart; counts → c-chart.
  2. Compute limits from a stable baseline
    Centre line ± 3 standard errors.
  3. Plot new points as they arrive
    Keep the limits fixed unless the process changes.
  4. Apply signal rules
    Point outside limits; runs of 8 on one side; trends.
  5. Investigate signals only
    Find the special cause; do not react to noise.
Cross Questions
Why ±3σ and not ±2σ?
With many weekly points, ±2σ would raise a false alarm about once every 20 weeks; ±3σ about once every 370 points.
Should the limits be recomputed every week including new data?
No; including special-cause points widens the limits and hides problems. Recompute only after a deliberate, verified process change.
How is a control chart related to hypothesis testing?
Each point is effectively a test of "the process is unchanged"; the chart adds time order and run rules that catch gradual shifts.
